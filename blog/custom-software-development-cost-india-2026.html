Custom software development cost in India (2026): real price ranges and red flags

The same brief can get quotes from ₹1 lakh to ₹15 lakh. These are the price ranges we see, what drives them, and the warning signs in a cheap quote.

Rajamuddin

CTO and Designated Partner, QivaLabs · Updated 23 Nov 2026

If you have collected quotes for a software project and the numbers make no sense, that's normal. We regularly meet business owners holding proposals from ₹1 lakh to ₹15 lakh for what sounds like the same system. Usually the cheap quote and the expensive one describe two very different pieces of software, and neither proposal says so clearly.

This guide covers what we see in the Indian market in 2026, from our side of the table as a software development company in Udaipur.

Price ranges by type of project

These ranges include design, development, testing and launch. They assume a competent team and code that someone else could maintain later.

Project Typical range in India What you get
Business website or landing page ₹25,000 to ₹1.2 lakh 5 to 15 pages, mobile-first, contact forms, basic SEO
Custom e-commerce store ₹1.5 lakh to ₹6 lakh Catalogue, cart, payments, shipping, order admin
Internal tool, portal or dashboard ₹2.5 lakh to ₹8 lakh Logins, roles, workflows, reports
Mobile app for Android and iOS ₹4 lakh to ₹15 lakh App, backend, admin panel, store launch
ERP or CRM built for your process ₹5 lakh to ₹20 lakh Stock, billing, sales, HR or support in one system
SaaS or multi-tenant platform ₹10 lakh to ₹40 lakh and up Subscriptions, tenant admin, integrations, compliance

A SaaS product at the top of that table is serious engineering. Expect six to twelve months before it is ready for paying customers.

What pushes the price up

The number of user roles matters more than the number of screens. An app where admins, staff and customers all see different things takes far more work than one where everyone sees the same screens.

Integrations are the second big cost. Connecting to Tally, a payment gateway, WhatsApp, a courier API or a government portal sounds like one line in a proposal. Each one has its own documentation, edge cases and test accounts.

Data migration is the third. If you are moving five years of records out of Excel or an old system, cleaning and importing that data can take weeks on its own.

The last is how certain the requirements are. A clear, written scope can be priced tightly. A vague one gets padded, because the team is pricing in the risk of not knowing what you want.

Red flags in a cheap quote

A low price isn't automatically bad, but these signs usually are:

  • No written scope. If the proposal is a price and a list of feature names, you will argue later about what each feature means.
  • Hourly rates that seem too good. Senior developers don't work for ₹200 an hour. A quote built on very junior developers often costs more in rework than it saved.
  • The vendor keeps the code. Make sure the source code, hosting accounts and domain are in your name.
  • Nothing about testing. If testing isn't a line item, it isn't happening.
  • Everything included, always. Software has trade-offs. A team that never says no either hasn't read your brief or will hand you surprises later.

How to get a quote you can trust

Write a one-page brief covering who uses the system, what they do in it, which existing tools it must connect to and what "done" looks like for the first version. Ask every vendor for a written scope, a timeline with milestones and a fixed price for that scope.

For bigger projects, a short paid discovery phase is worth it. In one or two weeks you get screen designs, a data model and a clear feature list. Even if you don't continue with the same company, you leave with a document any team can quote against. We run discovery before most custom builds, and for smaller projects we send a written scope and fixed quote within 48 hours.

Build or buy?

Before paying for custom software, check whether a ready product does 80 percent of what you need. For billing, school management or a simple CRM, a configured off-the-shelf tool is often cheaper and faster. Custom software makes sense when your process is what makes you money, or when every ready tool forces your team into workarounds.

We would rather tell you that than sell you a build you don't need. That's also why we built products like Qiva POS and School Connect: for common problems, a ready base saves our clients a lot of money.
